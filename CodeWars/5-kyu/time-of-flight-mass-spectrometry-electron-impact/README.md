<h2><a href=https://www.codewars.com/kata/6352fef8a06c1e18152ada76/train/python target="_blank">Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry: Electron Impact</a></h2><h3>5 kyu</h3><h1>Introduction</h1><p>Time of flight mass spectrometry is a useful method of analysing the mass of a substance. One of its uses and the one this kata will focus on is to find the <strong>relative abundances</strong> and <strong>m/z</strong> values of <strong>isotopes of an element</strong> in a sample. Henceforth the <strong>relative atomic mass</strong> may be calculated.</p><p>Note: If you already know about atomic mass, isotopes, relative abundances and relative atomic mass you can skip those sections.</p><p>And if you already know about time of flight mass spectrometry you can skip the section on that, but please read the section about the catch.</p><hr><h1>Basic Atomic Structure</h1><details><summary>Atomic Mass</summary><p>Atomic mass refers to the number of <strong>grams</strong> in <strong>one mole</strong> (6.022 x 10<sup>23</sup>) of the atom. For example, if the atomic mass of an atom were <strong>35</strong>, you would find that <strong>1.2044 * 10<sup>24</sup></strong> atoms had a mass of <strong>0.070kg</strong>.</p><p>An atomic mass of a <strong>specific isotope</strong> is always an integer. However, <strong>relative atomic masses</strong> are not always integers.</p></details><details><summary>Isotopes</summary><p>An <strong>isotope</strong> is a form of an element that contains an <strong>equal number of protons</strong> but a <strong>different number of neutrons</strong>. Put simply, it is a version of an element with a <strong>different atomic mass</strong> to other versions of the element. For example, chlorine atoms are usually said to exist in two isotopes: one with an atomic mass of <strong>35</strong>, and another with an atomic mass of <strong>37</strong>. (Note: this is not <em>exactly</em> true, but they are the most common by far). We can denote these two isotopes by writing <strong>Chlorine-35</strong> and <strong>Chlorine-37</strong> respectively. </p><p>When considering the <strong>natural</strong> isotopes, they tend to have atomic masses <strong>close to one another</strong>. They will definitely not differ by more than a factor of <strong>100%</strong> or more. That is, an element <strong>X</strong> will not exist in an isotope of <strong>X-10</strong> and <strong>X-20</strong> at the same time, for example. The only exception to this rule is <strong>Hydrogen</strong>, which has the natural isotopes of <strong>Hydrogen-1</strong> and <strong>Hydrogen-2</strong>.</p></details><details><summary>Relative Abundances</summary><p>Certain isotopes are more common than other isotopes. When I refer to <strong>relative abundances</strong>, I am referring to how often one isotope appears in a sample <strong>compared to other isotopes</strong> of the same element in a sample. For example, Chlorine-35 appears <strong>three times as often</strong> as Chlorine-37 in nature. We could have the relative abundance of Chlorine-35 in a sample be <strong>74%</strong> (meaning <strong>74%</strong> of the isotopes happened to be Chlorine-35), and the relative abundance of Chlorine-37 be <strong>26%</strong>, and this is a standard result and what gives Chlorine its <strong>relative atomic mass</strong> of <strong>35.5</strong> in the periodic table.</p></details><details><summary>Relative Atomic Mass</summary><p>The relative atomic mass of an element is the <strong>weighted average</strong> of the <strong>atomic masses</strong> of all its isotopes based on their <strong>relative abundances</strong>. <small>relative to the 1/12th the mass of the mass of a Carbon-12 atom, if you're being pedantic (or you're taking a chemistry exam ;))</small></p><p>For example, let's say in a sample we had <strong>74% of Chlorine-35</strong> and <strong>26% of Chlorine-37</strong>. Then the relative atomic mass would be <strong>74% * 35 + 26% * 37 = 35.52</strong>.</p></details><hr><h1>Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry</h1><details><summary>Time of Flight Mass Spectrometry</summary><p>A time of flight mass spectrometer can be split up into four stages:</p><ol>  <li>Ionisation</li>  <li>Acceleration</li>  <li>Flight</li>  <li>Detection</li></ol><p>In stage one, the isotopes in the sample are given a charge. For the small isotopes that we are considering in this kata, we will just use <strong>electron impact</strong>, which is when you fire a <strong>high energy electron</strong> at each atom in order to <strong>knock one of its electrons</strong> out of orbit, so it gains a <strong>positive charge</strong>. This does not change its <strong>atomic mass</strong>, since electrons have <strong>negligible atomic mass</strong> (~1/1800 atomic mass units).</p><p>In stage two, the now-charged atoms (technically not <strong>atoms</strong> since they are now charged, and are now called <strong>ions</strong> instead) are accelerated by an electric field of <strong>known strength</strong>. That is, each ion will gain the exact same <strong>kinetic energy</strong>.</p><p>In stage three, the moving ions will now travel through a flight tube of <strong>known length</strong>. The larger the <strong>atomic mass</strong> of the ion, the <strong>longer it will take</strong> to move through the flight tube.</p><p>In stage four, the charged ions hit a detector, and create a current. The <strong>magnitude of the current</strong> will determine the number of ions which hit it at the <strong>same time</strong> (and hence the number of ions with the <strong>same atomic mass</strong>).</p><p>Therefore, with all things considered, we can calculate the <strong>relative atomic mass</strong> of an element with a given sample, given we know the <strong>kinetic energy each ion was accelerated to</strong>, the <strong>magnitudes of the currents</strong> in the detector and the <strong>time</strong> that the currents were detected in. These will be the exact three inputs to your function.</p><p>Remember that the <strong>atomic mass</strong> of any isotope/ion should be a <strong>whole number</strong>. The calculations you perform may yield <strong>slightly inaccurate</strong> results because of the accuracy of the measurements taken by the spectrometer, but you should know to <strong>round</strong> these values.</p></details><details><summary>The Catch: Electron Impact</summary><p>The intention of electron impact is to give a <strong>1+</strong> charge to each ion (that is, <strong>one electron</strong> is knocked off orbit for each ion). However, rarely, <strong>two electrons</strong> may be accidentally knocked off orbit for some of the ions! This means that some ions may gain a <strong>2+</strong> charge! </p><p><strong>Double the charge</strong> means that the electric field gives the ion <strong>double the kinetic energy</strong>, which means it moves <strong>sqrt(2) as fast</strong> and has <strong>1/sqrt(2) the flight time</strong>! The ion appears to be <strong>twice</strong> as light as it actually is!</p><p>This is why the mass spectrometer is said to measure <strong>m/z</strong> (mass / charge) instead of just <strong>m</strong> (mass). We may find that there are some ions that appear to be <strong>twice as light</strong> as others since they hit the detector in <strong>1/sqrt(2) the time</strong>, when in fact it is <strong>not their mass</strong> that has changed, it is actually their <strong>charge</strong>.</p><p>But we know from the section on isotopes that two natural isotopes of the same element should <strong>never differ by that much</strong>! We know that this may happen for hydrogen, but hydrogen atoms <strong>only have one electron</strong>, so it is <em><strong>impossible for this mistake to happen for hydrogen atoms!</strong></em></p><p>So, your program should know to consider the detection of <strong>halved m/z values</strong> as the actual detection of the <strong>doubled m/z whole values</strong> in your calculation of the relative atomic mass.</p><p>Furthermore, even when the m/z values are <strong>halved</strong>, since the <strong>atomic mass</strong> is a whole number, the m/z values will always be a <strong>multiple of 0.5</strong>.</p><p><strong>Each isotope/ion</strong> that is detected may have an equivalent <strong>halved m/z value</strong> associated with it. There is <strong>not necessarily only one</strong> of these.</p></details><hr><h1>The Kata</h1><p>Your function <code>calculateRAM</code> will be given the following parameters in order:</p><ul>  <li>The <b>kinetic energy</b> (E) that the electric field accelerates all <b>1+</b> ions to, in <b>aJ (10<sup>-18</sup>J)</b></li>  <li>The <b>length</b> (d) of the flight tube, in <b>mm (10<sup>-3</sup>m)</b></li>  <li>An array of two-tuples (D), the first in each tuple representing the <b>magnitude of the current</b> (I) detected by the detector, in <b>µA (10<sup>-6</sup>A)</b>, and the second in each tuple repesenting the <b>time</b> (t) that the current was detected after, in <b>ns (10<sup>-9</sup>s)</b></li></ul><p>and you must return the <strong>relative atomic mass</strong> of the sample given.
